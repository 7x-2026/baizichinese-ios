Cangjie Creates Chinese Characters

It is said that the Chinese characters were first created by Cangjie. In fact, China's most beautiful script, full of patterns and lines, was probably developed over a long period of time by many people, each contributing their own wisdom. Perhaps during this long process, Cangjie made a particularly important contribution, and later people gave all the credit for inventing characters to him. Now, let's tell you the story about Cangjie creating characters.

In ancient times, there were no written characters. People remembered events in their minds. But human memory can't hold too much information. So, clever people came up with a method of tying knots on ropes to mark things. One knot meant one event; two knots meant two events. Big events were tied with big knots, and small ones with small knots. This helped people remember things like: "Yesterday, we hunted a mountain pig; tomorrow, we must go into the forest to gather fruit."

But after a long time, sometimes when they saw old knots, they couldn't remember what the event had been. So, others began using pictures to record things.

For example: yesterday I caught a black bear, so I drew a big bear on a piece of wood; I caught a wild rabbit, so I drew a rabbit...

It is said that Cangjie was the official in charge of records during the time of the Yellow Emperor. His job was to record all the major events happening in different parts of the world. Whether there was a war to record, a piece of land gained, a lunar eclipse, a flood or a drought—every event had to be written down. Even when the emperor performed rituals to worship heaven and earth, it had to be recorded. As the official in charge of records, Cangjie was probably the busiest person in the world. He would start working as soon as the sun rose, tying knots in ropes and drawing pictures to record everything, working until the sun set and still not resting—how tiring it must have been.

Cangjie didn’t mind the hard work, but what troubled him was that the pictures he drew were only understood by people around him. Others couldn’t understand them, and even less could they pass them on to future generations. For instance, he carefully drew a round sun, but someone else thought it was a full moon. How could anyone hope that future generations would understand such drawings?

Knowledge and experience cannot be passed on or taught to others—everything must be discovered through personal effort. So, at that time, humans lived in a very slow-progressing "dark age." Cangjie often bent over, tying knots in ropes or drawing pictures, murmuring to himself, "These things are so important—I wish I could make everyone understand them."

One spring morning, Cangjie was tired from work and his hands ached, so he went alone to the riverbank to rest. He sat on a stone, looking at the clear river water, still thinking about how he could record things in a way that everyone would understand. As he thought, he suddenly noticed a huge turtle crawling slowly and clumsily toward the shore. "Whoa! This turtle is enormous—it must be very old! Wait, there seem to be drawings on the turtle's back!" Cangjie approached curiously.

The big turtle's shell was red, possibly because it was very old. On its shell, covered with small hexagonal patterns, there were also long, greenish lines running across.

Cangjie looked carefully and realized that these green lines sometimes looked like mountain ranges rising and falling, sometimes like ripples of water flowing, and sometimes like straight tree trunks. After quietly observing for a long time, Cangjie suddenly slapped his thigh hard and shouted excitedly, "Ah! We can also use simple lines to draw the shapes of things! If we standardize how these images are drawn and teach them to people, even future generations will be able to understand them. Haha! That's wonderful! Then the sun will be clearly the sun, the moon will be clearly the moon, and no one will ever get them confused anymore!"

From that day on, Cangjie began to pay close attention to the surroundings: the shapes of the sun and moon, the forms of birds and animals, insects and fish, human movements, the shapes of tools and objects, and the postures of plants and trees... It is said that Cangjie had four eyes, so he observed things much more carefully than others. He noticed that every thing had its own unique features—really, the more he looked, the more interesting it became.

Next, Cangjie began to use lines to create symbols representing each thing.

For example, since the sunlight shines brightly, he used the "oracle bone script of 'sun'" to represent the sun; because the moon has a round shape that changes from full to empty each day, he simply used the "oracle bone script of 'moon'" to represent the moon; mountains rise and fall, so the "oracle bone script of 'mountain'" is just a mountain; water has ripples and waves, winding and twisting, so the "oracle bone script of 'water'" is simply water. Another example: using simple lines to draw a side view of a person standing, the "oracle bone script of 'person'" represents a person; and the "oracle bone script of 'deer'" represents a deer with antlers.

Cangjie became even happier as he created more. He then thought, what if I combine several images together to express more complex meanings? For example, the symbol for "sun rising in the morning" represents the sun rising over the horizon; the symbol for "a hand placing an arrow on a bow to shoot" shows someone shooting with a bow. Most wonderful of all, when these picture-like characters are written together in sequence, they can record complete events. For instance, writing "sun rising" followed by "a person shooting a deer" tells the full story: in the morning, someone shot a deer.

Thus, China's earliest pictographic script was invented.

From then on, Cangjie's work of recording important events in the world became much easier. He no longer had to spend all day tying knots on ropes or drawing pictures, working hard without rest. All he needed to do was simply draw simple lines to create characters, and he could express all kinds of meanings.

Later, because these pictographic characters were not difficult to learn, more and more people began to learn and use them. Some people built upon Cangjie's original creations, adding new characters or improving and simplifying old ones. Through the efforts of many generations, these characters gradually developed into the writing system we use today. Look carefully—do you see the early traces of the original characters in today's writing, such as "sun," "moon," "mountain," "water," "person," "deer," and "dawn"?

It is said that after Cangjie invented writing, a sudden shower of fragrant rice fell from the sky, as if celebrating the birth of writing and the fact that humans now had a tool to share knowledge and create civilization.

At night, once again, faint cries of ghosts and monsters could be heard from the darkness, as if mourning the fact that after writing was created, humans could escape the age of darkness and no longer be controlled by superstitions of ghosts and monsters, and could bravely move forward, forward.  

Perhaps Cangjie, with his four eyes, had already seen such a bright future. Shouldn't we today, who have our beautiful and progressive writing system, cherish and make good use of it?
