The Horse-Headed Silkworm

After the Yellow Emperor defeated Chiyou, in celebration of his victory, he ordered a piece of music to be composed, called the "Cangguo Tune," divided into ten chapters, such as "Thunder Shock," "Fierce Tiger Startled," "Spiritous Kui Roar," and "Bird-Eagle Struggle." Just from the names of these musical pieces, one can already imagine the bravery and grandeur. Accompanied by the special large drum known as the "Canggu," used during banquets for guests, the atmosphere was truly magnificent. Amid the booming drumbeats, victorious warriors sang songs of triumph and danced in various combat poses symbolizing battle victories on the hall floor. At that moment, sitting on the central throne in the hall, listening to the music and watching the dance, the Yellow Emperor's proud and triumphant mood could be easily imagined.

While the music was being played and everyone was happy, as a special addition to the celebration, a silk worm deity wearing a horse's skin descended gracefully from the sky, holding two strands of silk in hand—one strand yellow as gold, the other white as silver—coming to present them to the Yellow Emperor.

The goddess with the horse's skin was originally a beautiful young girl, only that she had this horse skin attached to her. This horse skin stuck to her body like roots, and had long since become one with her flesh, and there was no way to remove it. If she pulled the edges of the horse skin a little closer and wrapped it around her body, she would immediately turn into a silkworm—a silkworm with a horse's head. Even if she wished, she could instantly spit out long, shining silk from her mouth. In the wilds of the north, by the side of three tall, bare, branchless mulberry trees standing side by side, she was indeed half-kneeling and crawling up another tree, spinning silk day and night. People therefore called that wild area the "Silk Wild." Why is this beautiful girl covered in horse skin, transformed into a silkworm, and become the goddess of silkworms? There was once a folk tale like this.  

In ancient times, there was a man who went on a long journey and did not return home for a long time. His home had no one else but a little daughter and a male horse.

The male horse was fed by the little girl herself. The girl felt lonely at home and often longed for her father. One day, she playfully told the horse tied up in the stable: "Horse, if you can bring my father back to me, I will marry you."

Hearing this, the horse leaped up, snapped the reins, and jumped out of the stable, running straight into the yard. It ran day and night, without stopping, until it reached the place where her father lived. When the father saw his own horse arriving from afar, he was both surprised and joyful. But the horse behaved strangely—only looking toward the direction it had come from, stretching its neck and mourning with a mournful cry. The father thought to himself: "This horse has come from far away, yet it acts so strangely. Is something wrong at home?" So he didn't wait a moment, and quickly rode back home on the horse. When he got home, the daughter explained to her father: "Nothing bad has happened at home. We just miss you. The horse has feelings, so it went on its own to bring your father back."

Father said nothing, and stayed at home. Seeing how intelligent and emotionally attached the horse was, he felt very happy and treated it differently from before, always feeding it the finest food. But the horse showed no interest in the food, refused to eat, and every time he saw the little girl coming out of the yard, he would jump and call out, not just once.

Father noticed this behavior and felt puzzled, so he quietly asked his daughter, "Tell me, why does the horse jump and call whenever you come out?" The daughter had to honestly tell him about the time she played a trick on the horse. Upon hearing this, Father scowled and said to his daughter, "Oh, what a terrible thing! Don't tell anyone. From now on, you're not to leave this yard for the next few days."

Although Father loved the horse, he could not allow it to become his daughter's husband. To stop the horse from causing trouble, he secretly hid bows and arrows and shot the horse dead by the stable. Then he skinned the horse and hung the skin out in the yard.

That day, the father went out for business, and the little girl was playing with the girls from the neighboring homes beside a piece of horse leather. As soon as she saw the horse leather, she got angry and kicked it with her foot, shouting, "You beast! You think you can marry someone? Now you're stripped of your skin—deserved! See what happens to you…"

Before she could finish speaking, the horse leather suddenly leaped up from the ground, wrapped the little girl, and rushed toward the gate of the courtyard. It spun like the wind, and in a moment, it vanished into the distant fields. The girls from the neighborhood were terrified, scrambling and panicking, unable to save her. They could only wait for the father to return and tell him what had happened.

When the father heard the girls' account, he was deeply surprised. He searched everywhere nearby, but the girl was nowhere to be found.

A few days later, among the branches of a big tree, people found their daughter, who had been wrapped in horse skin, now transformed into a creeping, worm-like creature. Slowly swaying her horse-shaped head, she spat out a long, white, shining thread that wound itself around the tree branches. Curious people rushed to watch. They called this strange creature "silkworm," saying it spun thread to wrap itself; and they named the tree "mulberry," saying that young lives had been lost on this tree.

This is how the silkworm came to be. Later, the little daughter became the goddess of silkworms, and the horse skin remained on her body, forever her close and faithful companion.

After the Yellow Emperor defeated Chiyou, the silkworm goddess personally offered the silk she spun to the Yellow Emperor to celebrate his victory. The Yellow Emperor, seeing this beautiful and rare thing, greatly admired it, and ordered people to weave the silk into fabric. The resulting cloth was light and soft, like clouds floating in the sky or water flowing in a stream—far better than the old hemp cloth made before.

Cangjie's minister, Bo Yu, used the silk fabric to make clothes. The Yellow Emperor himself made ceremonial hats and robes from it. The Yellow Emperor's first wife, Leizu, the most honored heavenly goddess among all women, personally raised silkworms so they could spin beautiful silk like the Silkworm Goddess had given, weaving into soft, flowing silk fabrics. When Leizu first began raising silkworms, people all over the land began to follow her example. The silkworms multiplied and spread, eventually covering the entire land our ancestors once lived on. The work of raising silkworms, gathering mulberry leaves, and weaving cloth—this poetic labor—became the specialty of ancient Chinese women.
