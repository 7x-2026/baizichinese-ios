The Star Child, Part One

Long ago, two poor woodcutters were walking through a vast pine forest on their way home. It was a cold winter night, with biting wind. Thick snow covered the ground, and snow piled up on the branches. As they passed by, one after one, the small twigs on the sides broke off under the weight of the snow. When they reached the waterfall at the foot of the mountain, the waterfall stood still in mid-air, because the King of Ice had kissed it.

That night was incredibly cold—so cold that even animals and birds didn’t know what to do.

"Ah!" the wolf cried, shivering and dragging his tail as he limped out of the thicket, "What a miserable night!"

"Ooh! Ooh! Ooh!" the green plum bird chirped excitedly, "The old Earth has died. They've wrapped her in white funeral clothes and buried her."

"The Earth is getting married," the sparrows whispered to each other in secret. Their little red feet were frozen, but they felt it was their duty to look at everything with a cheerful, romantic spirit."

The weather truly was freezing.

The little squirrels living in the tall pine trees rubbed their noses against each other to stay warm. The rabbits huddled in their burrows, not daring to look out even once. The only creature that seemed happy about the weather was the great gull. Their feathers were stiff from the frost, but they didn’t care. They kept turning their large, bright yellow eyes and calling out to each other across the forest, "Tewit! Tewit! Tewit! Tewit! What a wonderful day the weather is!" Two woodcutters kept walking forward, blowing warm air into their fingers and hands. Their big boots with iron nails stepped over snow blocks. Once, they fell into a deep snow hole, and when they got out, they were as white as the grain grinder's workers, and the stones were slippery. Once, they fell off a hard, smooth ice patch formed from water in a swamp, and their bundles of firewood fell to the ground. They had to pick them up and tie them back together.

Once they thought they had lost their way, and their hearts grew terribly afraid, because they knew snow was cruel to those who slept in her arms. But they trusted Saint Martin, the god of travel, who would always look after anyone who set out on a journey, so they carefully retraced their steps, moving slowly and cautiously. Finally, they reached the exit of the forest and saw the lights of their village glowing far in the valley below.

When they realized they were safe again, they were overjoyed and burst into laughter. To them, the earth seemed like a silver flower, and the moon like a golden bloom.

But after laughing, they fell into sadness, remembering their poor lives. A woodcutter said to another, "Why should we be happy? Life is meant for the rich, not for people like us who are poor. It's better to freeze to death in the forest or be caught by some wild beast and eaten than to suffer like this."

"Indeed," his companion replied, "some people have too much, while others have too little. Injustice has already divided the world—no thing is fairly shared, except sorrow."

But just as they were sighing over their own misfortunes, something strange happened. A very bright and beautiful star fell from the sky. It passed by the other stars and slid down from the edge of the heavens, landing behind a cluster of willows not more than a bow's length from the sheep pen. 

"Ah! Whoever finds it will get a barrel of gold!" they shouted, rushing out, desperate to obtain the treasure.

One of them ran faster and overtook his companion, rushing through the thicket of willows and reaching the other side. There, on the snow, lay something that looked like gold.

He rushed over and bent down, reaching out to touch it. It was a cloak woven with golden threads, carefully embroidered with many stars, folded into several scrolls. He shouted loudly to his companion, "I've found treasure that fell from the sky!" When his friend approached, the two of them sat down on the snow and began to unfold the scrolls, ready to take out the gold and divide it equally. But oh no! Inside there was no gold, no silver, no treasure at all—only a sleeping child.

One of them said to the other, "Our hopes have ended in such a painful way. Our luck won't improve. What good is a child to one person? Let's leave here and go on our way. After all, we are both poor, each of us has our own children. We cannot share our child's bread with someone else."

But his companion replied, "No, it's wrong to leave the child out here to freeze to death. Even though I'm as poor as you are and have to feed several people, and there's nothing in the pot, I still want to take him home—my wife will take care of him."

He gently held the child in his arms, wrapped him in his cloak to keep him warm, and then went down the mountain back to the village. His companion was amazed by his foolishness and kindness.

When they arrived back in the village, his companion said to him, "Now that you have the child, give me your cloak—after all, we both know it should be shared equally."

But he replied, "No, because this cloak is not mine, nor is it yours—it belongs only to the child." He said goodbye to his companion and walked to his own door, knocking gently.

His wife opened the door and saw her husband safely returning to her side. She reached out, wrapped her arms around his neck, kissed him, and took a bundle of firewood from behind him, brushing the snow off his boots, telling him to hurry inside.

But he said to her, "I found something in the forest, and I brought it back so you can take care of it." He stood at the door and did not go in.

"What is it?" she asked loudly, "Show me! Our house is empty, and we need so many things!" He pulled back his cloak and held the sleeping child to her.

"Oh my heavens!" she murmured, "Do we not already have enough children? Why bring home a child found in the woods? Who knows—might he bring us misfortune? What will we feed him with?" She became angry with him.

"No, it's not like that," he replied. "He is a Star Child. I'll tell you about how I found him."

But she didn't let go of her anger—instead, she mocked him, angrily saying, "Our children don't even have bread to eat. Should we be feeding other people's children? Who will take care of us? Who will give us food?"  

"Don't say that," he replied. "God takes care of even sparrows. God feeds them."  

"Don't you know," she asked, "sparrows often starve in winter? Isn't it winter now?" Her husband had no answer and simply stood at the door, refusing to go inside. A cold wind blew in from the forest, rushing through the open door. She shivered and trembled, and said to him, "Don't you want to close the door? A cold wind has blown in, and I feel so cold."  

"Doesn't the wind blowing into a heart of iron always remain cold?" he asked back. She didn't answer, only moved closer to the fire.  

A while later, she turned to look at him, and tears filled her eyes.

He rushed in at once, placed the child in her arms, kissed him gently, then laid him on a small bed—where their youngest child slept.

The next day, the woodcutter took off the rare golden cloak and placed it in a large chest. His wife also removed the amber necklace worn around the child's neck and put it into the same chest.

Thus, the Star Child grew up alongside the woodcutter's children, sharing meals and playing together. Each year he grew more and more handsome. The people in the village were amazed, because everyone else had black skin and black hair, while he alone was fair and delicate, like fine ivory. His curly hair resembled a ring of narcissus flowers. His lips were like red petals, his eyes like purple violets by a clear river, and his body was as slender and graceful as water lilies growing in the fields before anyone had cut them down.

But his beauty brought him misfortune. He became proud, cruel, and selfish.

For the children of the woodcutter and the other kids in the village, he looked down on them all, saying they were born lowly, while he himself was noble—having come straight from the stars. He considered himself their master and called them all his slaves. He showed no sympathy for the poor, no pity for the blind, the disabled, or anyone suffering from illness. Instead, he threw stones at them or pushed them onto the roads, ordering them to go elsewhere and beg for alms. As a result, only the worst kind of people would return to the village for help a second time. He was also deeply obsessed with beauty, mocking the weak and ugly, treating them as nothing. But for himself, he was utterly devoted—when there was no wind in summer, he would lie by the well in the priest's orchard, gaze into the water, and admire the beauty of his own face, laughing happily at his own looks.

The woodcutter and his wife often blamed him, saying, "We have never treated you the way you treated the lonely and helpless. Why are you so cruel to those who need kindness and sympathy?"

The old priest also frequently went to him, trying to teach him love for all things, and said to him, "The flying fly is your brother. Don't harm it. The wild birds in the forest have their own freedom. Don't take pleasure in catching them. God created the snake and the mole, each with its own value. Who are you to bring pain into God's world? Even the animals on the farm know how to praise God."

But Star Child paid no attention to his words. He frowned, looked deeply unhappy, and walked back to find his companions to lead them in play.

His companions followed him, because he was beautiful, and his steps were light and graceful, able to dance, play the flute, and sing music. No matter where Star Child led them, they would go; no matter what he ordered, they would do it. When he stuck a sharp reed into the blurry eyes of a mole, they all laughed joyfully. When he threw stones at lepers, they joined in laughing. Whatever he commanded them to do, they would become as hard-hearted as he was.

One day, a poor beggar woman passed through the village. Her clothes were torn and tattered, and the long, rocky path had left her feet bleeding. She looked utterly miserable. Too tired to go on, she sat under a chestnut tree to rest.

Star Child saw her and said to his companions, "Look! Such a dirty beggar woman is sitting beneath that beautiful tree with green leaves. Come on, let's drive her away—she's truly ugly and annoying.

So he walked over and threw stones at her, mocking her. She looked at him with terrified eyes, staring straight at him. A woodcutter was chopping wood at the nearby hayfield and saw what the Star Child had done. He ran over and scolded him, saying, "Your heart is so cruel, with no mercy at all! What bad thing has this poor woman done to you that you treat her like this?"

The Star Child grew furious, his face turning red, and he stomped the ground with his foot, shouting, "Who are you to come and question me? I'm not your son, and I won't listen to you!"

"Exactly right," replied the woodcutter, "but when I first saw you in the forest, I too felt pity for you."

Hearing this, the woman screamed and then collapsed to the ground. The woodcutter carried her into his house, where his wife took care of her. When she woke up from her fainting, they brought her food and drink and told her to relax.

But she wouldn't eat or drink—she only asked the woodcutter, "Didn't you say the child was found in the forest? Wasn't it exactly ten years ago today?"

The woodcutter replied, "Yes, I found him in the forest, on this very day ten years ago."

"Did he have any marks when you found him?" she asked loudly. "Was there a string of amber necklace around his neck? Wasn't he wearing a golden robe embroidered with stars?"

"Yes, exactly," the woodcutter answered. "It was just as you described." He took out the robe and the amber necklace from the cabinet and showed them to her.

As soon as she saw these things, she burst into tears and cried, "He is my lost little son! Please hurry and call him here. I've traveled all over the world searching for him."

The woodcutter and his wife quickly went outside and called out, "Star Child! Come inside! You'll see your mother waiting for you there."

Star Child ran into the house full of wonder and joy. But when he saw that the person waiting for him was her, he laughed scornfully and said, "Hey, where is my mother? I only see this lowly beggar woman."

The woman replied, "I am your mother."

"You're crazy to say that!" Star Child shouted angrily. "I'm not your son because you're a beggar, and you're ugly and wearing rags. So get out of my sight—don't let me see your face again!"

"No, you are truly my little son," she shouted, suddenly falling to her knees and stretching out both arms. "Thieves took you from me and threw you into the forest, hoping you'd die. But as soon as I saw you, I recognized you. I still remember the signs: the golden-threaded cloak and the amber necklace. So please come with me—I've traveled all over the world searching for you."

"Come with me, my child, because I need your love."

But Star Child did not move an inch, showing no response to her words. All that could be heard was the woman's painful cries.

In the end, he finally spoke to her, his voice harsh and cruel. "If you truly are my mother," he said, "then you had better go away and never come back here to embarrass me. You know I believed I was a child of some star, not a beggar's child, as you just told me. So go away—never let me see you again."

"Oh! My son!" she screamed loudly, "Didn't you even want to kiss me once before you leave? Because I've gone through so much suffering to find you!"

"No," said Star Child, "you are too ugly. I would rather kiss a venomous snake or a toad than kiss you."

So the woman got up and cried sadly, then walked back into the forest. When Star Child saw her go, he was happy and ran back to his friends, ready to play with them.

But when they saw him coming, they all laughed and mocked him, "You're as ugly as a toad, and as dangerous as a venomous snake! Get away from us—no one can stand being with you!" And they pushed him out of the garden.

Star Child frowned and said to himself, "What are they talking about? I must go to the well and look at myself there. The well will tell me how beautiful I really am."

He went to the well and looked into its water. Oh! His face was just like a toad's, and his body was as long and snake-like as a poisonous snake. He fell to the ground and began to cry, whispering to himself, "This must be the punishment for my sins. I didn't recognize my own mother, and I drove her away—brutal and proud. That's why I am like this."

"So I must go. I must travel all over the world to find her. I won't rest until I find her and she forgives me."

At that moment, the little daughter of the woodcutter came close to him. She placed her hand on his shoulder and said, "It doesn't matter if you've lost your beauty. Stay with us. I won't mock you."

He replied, "No, I have been too cruel to my mother. This punishment is the consequence of my wrongdoing. So I must leave right away and travel all over the world to find my mother, until I find her and earn her forgiveness."

So he ran into the forest, calling out to his mother, begging her to come back to him. But there was no answer. All day long he called her, and when the sun set, he lay down on a bed made of leaves. The birds and animals saw him and all fled, remembering his cruelty. He was alone, with only the toad looking at him, and the slow-moving snake crawling past him.

In the morning he got up and picked several bitter grass plums from the tree, then walked through the great forest forward, crying sadly. No matter what he encountered, he would go up and ask, "Have you seen my mother?"

He said to the mole, "You can go down into the earth. Tell me, is my mother there?"

The mole replied, "You have already blinded my eyes. How could I possibly know?"

He then said to the plum bird, "You can fly high above the tallest trees and see the whole world. Tell me, can you see my mother?"

The plum bird answered, "You have already cut off my wings for fun. How could I possibly fly?"

To the little squirrel who lived alone in a cypress tree, he said, "Where is my mother?"

The little squirrel replied, "You have already killed my mother. Do you also want to kill your own mother?"

Star Child cried, lowered his head, and begged the creatures made by God to forgive him, asking them to let him keep going through the forest and find the woman who was his mother. On the third day, he reached the end of the forest and arrived on the plain.

As he passed through the village, the children mocked him and threw stones at him. The villagers wouldn't let him sleep in their granaries, fearing he would spoil the stored grain. They were so afraid of him that they pushed him away. No one in the village showed him any sympathy. He heard no word at all about the woman who was his mother. Though he had searched every corner of the world for three years, he still felt as though she was walking ahead of him, always just out of reach. He often called out to her, chasing after her, until his feet were bleeding from the sharp stones. But he never caught up with her. The people living by the roadside said they had never seen her, or a woman like her. They only laughed at his sorrow.

For three years he had traveled all over the world, and in this world he found neither love, nor care, nor kindness—yet this very world had once been the one he had created for himself.  

One night he arrived at the city gate of a city surrounded by strong walls, located by a river. He was tired, and his feet ached, but he still entered the city. However, the soldiers stationed there blocked his way, speaking harshly to him: "What are you doing here?"  
"I am looking for my mother," he replied. "I beg you to let me enter the city—perhaps she lives here."  

They mocked him. One of them, playing with his black beard, lowered his shield and shouted loudly: "Honestly, if your mother sees you like this, she won't be happy. You're worse than a toad from a swamp or a poisonous snake crawling through the mud. Get out! Get out! Your mother does not live in this city."

Another soldier, holding a yellow flag, said to him, "Who is your mother? Why are you looking for her?"  

He replied, "My mother is also a beggar, just like me. I treated her badly. I ask you to let me enter so that she may forgive me. If she really lives in this city, please let me go in." But they still wouldn't let him enter, and they stabbed him with their spears.  

So the Star Child had to cry and turn around and leave. At that moment, a man came walking up. He wore armor decorated with golden flowers, and on his helmet sat a lion with wings. He asked the soldiers, "Who asked to enter the city?"  

The soldiers answered, "A beggar. His mother is also a beggar. We've already kicked him out."  

"No," the man laughed loudly, "we could sell this ugly fellow as a slave. His value would be worth a cup of sweet wine."  

At that moment, an old man with a ugly appearance passed by and shouted, "I'll pay a price to buy him."

After he paid the money, he took Star Child's hand and led him into the city.

They walked down several streets and reached a small door that was set into a wall beneath a lychee tree. The old man tapped the door gently with a carved jade ring, and the door opened. They stepped down five copper steps and entered a garden filled with black poppies. There were many green porcelain jars scattered about. The old man took a silk-handled handkerchief from his headcloth, tied it tightly over Star Child's eyes, and pushed him forward as he walked ahead. When the silk handkerchief was removed from Star Child's eyes, he found himself in a dungeon, where a cowhorn lamp was burning.

The old man placed a wooden tray with a moldy piece of bread in front of Star Child and said, "Eat," and then filled a cup with salty water and said, "Drink." After Star Child had eaten and drunk, the old man left, locked the door, and fastened it with an iron chain.

The next day, the old man came to see him again. This old man was indeed one of the most skilled magicians among the Libyans, and his powers had been learned from a master who lived in a tomb along the Nile River. The old man frowned and said to him, "Near the city gate of this evil cult, in a forest, there are three gold coins. One is made of white gold, one is yellow, and the third is red. Today you must bring back the white one. If you can't, I'll whip you one hundred times. Hurry off and go—by sunset, I'll be waiting for you at the garden gate. Remember: you must bring back the white coin, or you'll be in trouble, because you are my slave, and I bought you for a bowl of sweet wine." He then tied the silk-patterned handkerchief over the Star Child's eyes, led him out of the house, through this garden full of poppies, and up the five bronze steps. After using his ring to open the small door, he set the Star child free on the street.

So Star Child stepped out of the city gate and entered the forest the magician had told him about.

From the outside, the forest looked absolutely beautiful, as if everywhere there was bird song and blooming flowers. Star Child excitedly walked inside. But the beauty of the forest didn't bring him any benefit—no matter where he tried to go, sharp, thick thorns sprang up from the ground, blocking his path. Ugly nettles stung him, and thistles used their sharp spines to pierce him, making him suffer unbearable pain. And all over the place, he couldn't find the piece of white gold the magician had mentioned. Though he searched from morning until noon, and then from noon until sunset. After sunset, he had no choice but to turn around and go back, crying all the way, because he knew what fate was waiting for him.

But just as he reached the edge of the forest, he heard a painful cry coming from somewhere deep inside. Instantly forgetting his worries, he ran toward the sound. He saw a little rabbit trapped in a hunter's snare.

The Star Child felt sympathy for it and set it free, saying, "I myself am also a slave, but I can return you to your own."

The rabbit replied, "You have already given me back myself. What can I give you in return?"

The Star Child said, "I am looking for a piece of white gold, but I can't find it anywhere. If I can't bring it back to my master, he will beat me."

"Come with me," the rabbit said, "I will take you there, because I know where it is hidden and why it is hidden there."

So the Star Child and the rabbit went together. Ah! In the crack of an old oak tree, he saw the white gold he was looking for. He was thrilled and grabbed it, saying to the rabbit, "You have already repaid me a hundred times for the little thing I did for you. The small kindness I showed you, you have returned a hundredfold."

"No, that's not true," the rabbit replied. "I'm just returning to you the way you treated me." Saying that, the rabbit ran away, and Star Child walked toward the city.

At the city gate sat a leper, his face covered by a green cloth headband. His eyes, like burning coals, shone through small holes in the cloth. When he saw Star Child approaching, he tapped a wooden bowl and shook his bell, calling out, "Give me a coin, or I'll starve to death. The people have driven me out of the city and no one has shown me any mercy."

"Oh no!" Star Child exclaimed, "I only have one coin in my wallet. If I don't give it to my master, he'll beat me, because I'm his slave."

But the leper kept clinging to him, begging him, and finally, moved by pity, Star Child gave him the white coin.

When Star Child returned to the magician's room, the magician opened the door and let him in. He said to him, "Did you get the white coin?" Star Child answered, "No, I didn't get it." At once, the magician rushed at him and hit him. He placed an empty wooden plate in front of him and said, "Eat," then gave him an empty cup and said, "Drink." Then he pushed him back into the dungeon.

The next day, the magician came to him again and said, "If you cannot bring me back the golden coin today, I will make you my slave forever and whip you three hundred times."

So Star Child went back into the forest. All day long he searched for the golden coin, but he couldn't find it anywhere. When the sun set, he sat down and began to cry. At that moment, the little rabbit came running again—the one he had rescued from the trap.

The rabbit asked him, "Why are you crying? What are you looking for in the forest?"

"Star Child" responds, "I'm looking for a golden coin, and it's hidden right here. If I can't take it back with me, my master will beat me and treat me like a slave."

"Come with me," the rabbit shouts, rushing through the forest until it reaches a pond. The golden coin lies at the bottom of the pool.

"I don't know how to thank you," says Star Child. "By the way, this is the second time you've saved me."

"No, it's not. It was you who first showed me kindness," the rabbit says, and then dashes away quickly.

Star Child takes the golden coin and puts it in his wallet, rushing toward the city. But as he approaches, the leper runs out and meets him, falling to his knees before him, crying, "Give me a coin, or I'll starve to death."

Star Child says to him, "In my wallet, I have only one golden coin. If I don't give it to my master, he will beat me and keep me as a slave."

However, the leper still begged desperately, so Star Child felt pity and gave him the golden coin again.

When he returned to the magician's house, the magician opened the door and let him in, asking, "Did you get the golden coin?" Star Child replied, "No, I didn't get it." At once, the magician rushed at him, beat him, chained him with a chain, and threw him into the dungeon.

On the third day, the magician came to him and said, "If you bring me back the red gold coin today, I'll set you free. But if you can't bring it back, I'll definitely kill you."

So Star Child went back into the forest and searched all day for the red gold piece, but he couldn't find it anywhere. In the evening, he sat down and began to cry. As he cried, a little rabbit appeared before him.

The rabbit said to him, "The red gold coin you're looking for is inside the cave behind you."

"So you don't need to cry anymore—you should be happy instead."

"How can I ever repay you?" said Star Child loudly, "Ah, this is already the third time you've saved me."

"No, it's you who were the first to show compassion toward me," said the rabbit, and then hurried away.

Star Child entered the cave and in the deepest corner found the red coin. He put it into his wallet and quickly returned to the city. The leper saw him coming and stood in the middle of the road, crying loudly and shouting, "Give me that red coin right away, or I will die!" Star Child felt compassion again and gave him the red coin, saying, "Your need is greater than mine." But at that moment his heart was heavy, because he knew what terrible fate was waiting for him.

Yet! As he passed by the city gate, the guards all bowed to him and said, "How beautiful our emperor is!"

A group of citizens followed him, shouting loudly, "There is no one in the entire world more beautiful than him!" Star Child began to cry, and whispered to himself, "They're mocking me again, making fun of my misfortune." More and more people gathered, and he lost his way in the crowd. In the end, he found himself at a huge square—right in front of the royal palace.

The palace gates opened, and monks and ministers came out to welcome him. They bowed respectfully and said, "You are the emperor we have been waiting for. You are the son of the king."

Star Child answered them, "I am not the king's son. I am the child of a poor beggar woman. Why do you say I am beautiful? I know how ugly I look."

At that moment, a man with golden floral decorations on his armor and a lion with wings perched on his helmet, holding a shield, stepped forward and shouted loudly, "How could my emperor say he is not beautiful?"

Star Child looked up—ah!

His own face was once again just as it had been before, and his beauty had returned to what it once was. And now, he saw in his eyes something he had never seen before.

The monks and ministers bowed before him and said, "An ancient prophecy once said that today a person would come to rule over us. So, please, let our king accept this crown and this royal staff, and rule us with justice and kindness."

But he replied, "I am not worthy. For I do not recognize my own mother, and until I find her and gain her forgiveness, I will not rest. So let me go. I will travel once again across the world. I will not stay here, no matter how much you offer me the crown and the staff."

After saying this, he turned around and walked toward the street leading to the city gate. Look! Among the crowd pressing around the soldiers, he saw his mother, standing beside her was the leper. And he stood right in the middle of the road.

He suddenly cried out with excitement and ran toward them, kneeling down to kiss the wound on his mother's foot, using his tears to wash it clean. He lowered his head into the dust, weeping like a broken heart, and said to her: "Mother, when I was proud, I did not recognize you. Now, when I am lowly, accept me. Mother, I once treated your kindness as hatred—now give me your love. Mother, I once rejected you—now please take my child as yours." But the beggar woman did not answer him a single word.

He then reached out his hands and grasped the pale feet of the leper, saying: "I have shown compassion to you three times. Please ask my mother to say one word to me." But the leper also did not answer him a single word.

He began to cry again and said, "Mother, my pain is too great to bear. Please forgive me. Let me go back to the forest."  
The woman who was begging placed her hand on his head and said, "Get up."  
The leper also placed his hand on his head and said, "Get up."  

He stood up and looked at them. Oh! They were really the King and the Queen.  

The Queen said, "This is your father, whom you once saved."  

The King said, "This is your mother, whom you once washed your feet with tears."  

They bent down and hugged his neck, kissed him, and took him into the palace. They dressed him in beautiful clothes and placed a crown on his head, gave him a staff in his hand. From that day on, he ruled over the city by the river and became its master. He showed great fairness and kindness to everyone. He drove away the evil magician and gave many treasures to the woodcutter and his wife, and bestowed great honor upon their children.

He could not tolerate anyone mistreating birds or animals, and he taught the people love, kindness, and forgiveness. He gave bread to the poor and clothes to those who were naked. In this kingdom, peace and prosperity flourished.
