The Wooden Girl

On the endless green grasslands of Mongolia, there flows a clear little river, its water gently moving forward, reflecting colorful sunsets, sunlight, or moonlight, showing different scenes at any moment. By the beautiful riverbank lives an old carpenter and an old painter.

The old carpenter has superb skills—no matter how rough the wood, once shaped by him, it becomes a delicate dragon or a beautiful phoenix. When the old painter adds colorful brushstrokes, the dragon and phoenix come to life, only needing a little more to fly off into the sky. For decades, the two have worked side by side—one carving, one painting, living together as a team.

But now they are old, with no children or grandchildren, and feel very lonely and isolated. One evening, as they sat by the riverbank, they suddenly saw a long, thick piece of wood floating in the water. They quickly worked together to pull it out. The old carpenter examined it carefully and had a brilliant idea. He said to the old painter, "This piece of wood is perfect—I'll carve a little girl to be a companion!" The old painter happily agreed, "That's wonderful!"

"The girl is so cute," said the old carpenter, "let's carve a little girl. I'll paint her colors!"  

So the old carpenter used his most skillful hands to carefully carve each detail—the big eyes, the small mouth, the cute upturned nose—piece by piece, one by one, from his hands.  

The old painter also put in his finest effort, painting colors on the girl. Her bright red dress seemed to wave in the wind, her soft, pink face looked as if it could be gently touched, and her two long black braids shone beautifully. The beads around the brim of her hat, and every other part, were all so lovely.  

The girl was now fully carved and painted. She looked so real, as if she could just breathe and move. The two old craftsmen placed her by the edge of a small river and named her "Wooden Girl."  

Since the Wooden Girl came into being, the old carpenter and the old painter have been much happier. Every morning before they leave for work, they say goodbye to her. Every evening when they return, they greet her. It has become a daily habit for them both.

But every time they went out to work, all that remained by the small river was the lonely wooden girl—no companion except the wind blowing across the grasslands and the occasional herd of cows and sheep passing by her side.

One day, the old carpenter and the old painter left again, and suddenly a young boy on horseback appeared on the grasslands. He had no money, only a horse, a sincere heart, and a beautiful singing voice.

From a distance, he saw a beautiful girl standing by the riverbank. Without hesitation, he kicked his horse's side and galloped toward her, gently calling, "Girl! Girl!" But she didn't respond. He dismounted and approached carefully, only to find that the girl was actually a wooden figure.

The boy felt a little disappointed, but soon he opened his voice and sang warmly: "Beautiful girl, as soon as you appear, the stars in the sky stop shining, and the birds below cease to sing. Girl, girl, please come back to life! I'll sing a song for you, and I'll take you galloping across the grasslands. Girl, girl, please come back to life!"

The boy's clear song rang across the plain. A gentle breeze blew, and the corners of the wooden girl's dress fluttered softly. Her eyes sparkled with happy light, and a gentle smile appeared on her lips—she had truly come to life like a real person. The boy jumped excitedly, grabbed her hand, and spun her three times before lifting her onto a horse and galloping joyfully across the vast grassland.

The boy took the girl to see the clouds, the flowing rivers, and when the sun dipped toward the west, he showed her the moon and the stars. The girl was absolutely delighted.

At that moment, the old carpenter and the old painter returned home and found the wooden girl was gone. They were deeply worried and searched everywhere, only to finally spot the wooden girl dancing and singing with a young boy on the open grassland.

The two old men looked at each other and shouted with joy, "Our wooden girl has come to life! Our wooden girl has come to life!" They rushed over, wanting to take the girl back from the boy. Of course, the boy refused, and the two old men began to argue.

The royal carriage happened to pass by, and when the king saw a young boy and two old men arguing under the moonlight, he sent men to bring them before him and questioned them. They shouted and complained, telling the king about what had happened. As soon as the king saw the beautiful and lovely wooden girl, he couldn't help thinking of bad ideas and loudly said to them, "Enough! You live on my land, eat the grain grown on my land, and carve this girl from wood grown on my land—this girl belongs to me!" After saying this, the king ordered the guards to take the wooden girl and put her onto the carriage, and they rushed off quickly. The young boy tried to chase after them but couldn't catch up, and the two old men called out, but no one came back.

After being taken into the palace, the girl remained silent and unhappy. The king ordered food to be brought to her, but she wouldn't eat it. Fine clothes were made for her, but she wouldn't wear them. No one in the palace spoke to her, nor did anyone sing for her or take her to see the clouds or ride a horse. The girl felt very lonely, her eyes slowly lost their shine, her smile disappeared, and finally she turned back into a cold, hard piece of wood.

The king brought the old carpenter, the old painter, and the young boy to the palace and ordered them: "Quick, bring the wooden girl to life! I will reward you with gold and silver!" The old carpenter and the old painter looked helpless and said: "We only know how to carve wood. We don't have the power to bring wood to life!"

The young boy looked sadly at the wooden girl and angrily said to the king: "It's all your fault—the girl turned back into wood! Give her back to me! Don't let her suffer in the palace!"

The wooden girl heard this and tears shone in her eyes. But the king, furious, sent the old carpenter and the old painter out of the palace and cruelly locked the young boy in the palace prison.

Every day, seeing the cold, hard wooden girl, the king grew angry. Whenever he passed by her, he would hit her or kick her. In the end, he decided to burn her.

A fierce fire broke out in the palace. The wooden girl was placed on the pile of wood. The flames grew stronger and stronger, lighting up her face, turning it red.

As the fire neared the wooden girl, the flames suddenly spread sideways and climbed onto the palace, setting the king's clothes on fire. Terrified, the king rushed out of the palace.

The fire broke into the prison and destroyed the prison gate. The young man did not run away immediately. Instead, he charged into the palace through the flames, rescued the wooden girl from the pile of firewood, took a horse, and fled with her from the royal palace. But at this moment, the young man's clothes were torn and burned beyond recognition, and the wooden girl's arms were scorched by the flames, leaving two deep burns. Her painted makeup had also fallen off. Yet the young man still held her tightly, caring for her, and ran desperately back to the great grassland.

Old carpenter and old painter were sitting sadly by the small river. When they saw the young man and the girl return, they were so happy! They immediately picked up their tools to repair the wooden girl's arms and painted new makeup on her. The wooden girl looked just as beautiful as before. Only she stood motionless, silent and still.

The young boy sadly called out to the wooden girl, singing the song from long ago. When he sang, "Girl, girl, please come back to life..." the wooden girl blinked her eyes and shed a string of tears—she had truly come back to life.

From then on, the young boy often took the revived wooden girl and joyfully rode across the vast grasslands, watching the clouds, the flowing rivers, and the setting sun. They lived together with the old carpenter and the old painter, never separating again.
